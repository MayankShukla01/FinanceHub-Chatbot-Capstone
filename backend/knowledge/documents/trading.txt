# Trading in the Indian Stock Market

## Intraday Trading vs Delivery Trading

### Delivery Trading (CNC — Cash and Carry)
- You buy shares and they are delivered to your Demat account
- You hold the shares for as long as you want (days, months, years)
- Settlement: T+1 (shares delivered next trading day)
- No leverage — you need the full amount to buy
- Suitable for long-term investors
- On Zerodha: Select "CNC" product type
- Brokerage: Usually zero for delivery trades on discount brokers

### Intraday Trading (MIS — Margin Intraday Square-off)
- You buy and sell shares on the same trading day
- All positions must be closed (squared off) before market closes (3:15 PM auto square-off on most brokers)
- Shares never enter your Demat account
- You can get leverage (margin) — trade with more money than you have
- Higher risk, requires active monitoring
- On Zerodha: Select "MIS" product type
- Brokerage: ₹20 per trade or 0.03% (whichever is lower) on most discount brokers

### NRML (Normal)
- Used for Futures and Options trading
- Positions can be carried forward until expiry
- Requires margin money

## Understanding Stop Loss Orders

A Stop Loss (SL) order is designed to limit your losses on a trade. It automatically triggers a sell order when the stock price falls to a specified level.

**Types of Stop Loss:**

### Stop Loss Market (SL-M)
- You set a trigger price
- When the stock hits the trigger price, a market order is placed
- Guaranteed execution but not guaranteed price
- Example: You bought at ₹100, set SL-M trigger at ₹95 — if price drops to ₹95, shares are sold at market price

### Stop Loss Limit (SL)
- You set both a trigger price and a limit price
- When the trigger is hit, a limit order is placed at the limit price
- Not guaranteed to execute if the price moves too fast
- Example: Trigger at ₹95, Limit at ₹94 — if price drops to ₹95, a sell order is placed at ₹94 or better

### Trailing Stop Loss
- Stop loss that moves with the stock price
- As the stock goes up, the stop loss moves up automatically
- Locks in profits while protecting against downside
- Not all brokers support this natively

## Order Types in Detail

### Market Order
- Executes immediately at the best available price
- Use when: You want instant execution and don't mind small price differences
- Risk: In volatile or illiquid stocks, you may get a worse price than expected (slippage)

### Limit Order
- Executes only at your specified price or better
- Buy limit: Executes at your price or lower
- Sell limit: Executes at your price or higher
- Use when: You want a specific price and are willing to wait

### GTT (Good Till Triggered) Order
- A long-term order that remains active until a trigger price is reached
- Available on Zerodha and some other brokers
- Valid for 1 year
- Use when: You want to buy/sell at a future price without monitoring daily
- Example: Set a GTT to buy Reliance if it drops to ₹2,200 — even if it takes months, the order stays active

### Bracket Order
- Combines three orders in one: Entry order + Target order + Stop Loss order
- When the entry order executes, both target and stop loss are placed automatically
- If one triggers, the other is cancelled
- Used by intraday traders for disciplined trading
- Available only for intraday (MIS) on most brokers

### AMO (After Market Order)
- Orders placed outside market hours (after 3:30 PM or before 9:00 AM)
- Queued and executed when the market opens next day
- Useful if you can't trade during market hours

## T+1 Settlement

In India, the stock market follows T+1 (Trade plus 1 day) settlement:
- **T (Trade day)**: The day you buy or sell shares
- **T+1**: Shares are delivered to your Demat account (if buying) or money is credited to your bank (if selling) the next trading day

Earlier, India followed T+2 settlement. The move to T+1 in 2023 made Indian markets one of the fastest settling markets in the world.

## Futures and Options (F&O) Basics

F&O are derivative instruments whose value is derived from an underlying asset (usually a stock or index like Nifty 50).

### Futures
- A contract to buy or sell an asset at a predetermined price on a future date
- **Obligation** to buy/sell — you must settle the contract
- Traded in lots (e.g., Nifty futures lot size = 25 units)
- Requires margin money (typically 10-15% of contract value)
- Expires on the last Thursday of the month (monthly) or week (weekly for Nifty)
- Can be used for hedging or speculation
- High leverage = high risk

**Example**: You buy 1 lot of Nifty futures (25 units) at 22,000. If Nifty goes to 22,500, your profit = (22,500 - 22,000) × 25 = ₹12,500. If it drops to 21,500, your loss = ₹12,500.

### Options
- A contract that gives you the **right but not obligation** to buy or sell
- Two types:
  - **Call Option (CE)**: Right to buy at a specific price (strike price)
  - **Put Option (PE)**: Right to sell at a specific price (strike price)
- **Premium**: The price you pay to buy an option
- **Strike Price**: The price at which you can exercise the option
- Maximum loss for option buyer = Premium paid
- Maximum loss for option seller = Unlimited (theoretically)

**Example**: Nifty is at 22,000. You buy a 22,200 Call Option (CE) for ₹100 premium. If Nifty goes to 22,500, your option is worth ₹300 (22,500 - 22,200), so your profit = ₹300 - ₹100 (premium) = ₹200 per unit.

**WARNING**: F&O trading is very risky. SEBI data shows that 9 out of 10 individual F&O traders lose money. Beginners should avoid F&O until they have significant market experience.

## Margin Trading

Margin trading means trading with borrowed money from your broker. It allows you to buy more shares than your available cash.

- **Margin**: The amount you need to deposit to take a position
- **Leverage**: The ratio of total position to your margin
- Example: With 5x leverage, ₹20,000 margin lets you trade ₹1,00,000 worth of shares
- Available for intraday and F&O trading
- **Risk**: Losses are also magnified — you can lose more than your investment
- **Margin call**: If your losses exceed a threshold, the broker asks you to deposit more money or closes your position

## Short Selling

Short selling means selling shares you don't own, betting that the price will fall.

### Intraday Short Selling
- Sell first, buy later (within the same day)
- If the price drops, you profit from the difference
- Available for all stocks in intraday (MIS) mode
- Must square off before market close

### Delivery Short Selling
- Very risky and not recommended for beginners
- Requires borrowing shares through Securities Lending and Borrowing (SLB)
- Additional costs involved

## Candlestick Chart Basics

Candlestick charts show price movement over a time period. Each "candle" shows four data points:

1. **Open**: Price at which the stock started trading in that period
2. **Close**: Price at which the stock ended trading
3. **High**: Highest price reached during the period
4. **Low**: Lowest price reached during the period

**Green/White candle**: Close > Open (price went up — bullish)
**Red/Black candle**: Close < Open (price went down — bearish)

The thick part is called the "body" and the thin lines are called "wicks" or "shadows."

Common candlestick patterns beginners should know:
- **Doji**: Open and close are nearly equal — shows indecision
- **Hammer**: Small body at top, long lower wick — potential reversal from downtrend
- **Engulfing**: A candle that completely engulfs the previous one — strong reversal signal

## Volume

Volume refers to the total number of shares traded during a specific period.

- **High volume**: Many shares traded — indicates strong interest and conviction
- **Low volume**: Few shares traded — indicates weak interest
- **Volume confirms trends**: A price rise on high volume is more reliable than on low volume
- **Delivery percentage**: The percentage of traded shares that were actually delivered (not squared off intraday). High delivery % suggests genuine buying interest.
